The wrong target

Grind size is the quickest lever you have when a brew tastes wrong. If the cup is sour, thin or salty, the water has not extracted enough, so grind finer or brew a little longer. If it is bitter, harsh or hollow, the water has extracted too much, so grind coarser or shorten the brew. Make one change at a time.

Harbor & Pine began in a rented boathouse where the first roaster, a secondhand drum machine, shared floor space with coils of rope and a bench of old tools. Every morning the smell of toasting beans drifted across the water and drew fishermen in from the dock. They asked for a cup, then a bag, and within a year the little shop had a line out of the door before sunrise.

A burr grinder is the single most useful upgrade for home brewing. Blades chop beans into uneven pieces, so some particles over extract and turn bitter while others under extract and taste sour. Burrs crush the beans between two abrasive surfaces and produce a far more consistent size, which means the water can do its work evenly and the cup tastes balanced.

Why it matters

Light roasts preserve the character of the origin, with fruit, flowers and tea-like acidity showing up clearly. Medium roasts trade a little of that brightness for caramel sweetness and a rounder body. Dark roasts bring bold, smoky flavours and low acidity, but the roasting itself becomes the main flavour. None of them is wrong; they simply suit different moods and brewing methods.

Behind the counter our team keeps a notebook where every roast is logged: charge temperature, development time, the weather, and a short tasting comment. Over several years this record has become the most valuable asset in the building, because it lets us repeat a great roast on demand and understand exactly why an unexpected batch turned out differently.
