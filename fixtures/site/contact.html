Café culture here has always been social. People arrive with laptops, notebooks, dogs and children, and the tables fill up with conversation. We try to protect that atmosphere by keeping music low, offering a quiet corner near the back, and never rushing anybody who wants to linger over a second cup while the rain passes.

Freshness matters more than most people expect. Coffee is at its best between one and four weeks after roasting, once the trapped gas has settled but before the aromatic oils fade. That is why every bag we sell carries the roast date rather than a vague best before stamp, and why we roast in small batches several times a week instead of stockpiling inventory.

The pour over method rewards a steady hand. Wet the filter first to remove any papery taste, add your grounds, and pour a small amount of hot water to let the coffee bloom. After thirty seconds, continue pouring in slow circles until you reach your target weight. The whole process should take about three minutes and produces a clear, delicate cup.

Every Saturday we run a free public cupping at ten in the morning. We lay out six small bowls of freshly ground coffee, pour hot water over each, and slurp from a spoon to taste the differences. It sounds strange, but it is the quickest way to learn what you enjoy, and the regulars have become some of our best teachers.

We roast on a small electric machine that recirculates its own hot air, which cuts our energy use and keeps the smoke down in a street full of houses. Neighbours sometimes stop by just to say how pleasant the aroma is, and we take that as a better review than any online rating.
