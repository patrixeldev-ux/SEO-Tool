Gift boxes are popular in winter. Each one holds three small bags of different roasts, a printed tasting card, and a hand stamped paper sleeve. We include a note describing the farm behind every coffee, so the person opening the box learns something about the people who grew it before they even take the first sip.

Roasting is mostly patience. Beans arrive hard and pale green, then turn yellow, then a soft tan, and finally begin to crack as moisture escapes. A good roaster listens for that first crack, watches the colour, and adjusts the flame so the sugars develop without scorching. Rushing the process leaves a grassy sourness, while pushing too far burns away everything that made the bean special.

Cold brew is forgiving and easy to make at home. Combine coarsely ground coffee with cold water, stir gently, and leave the jar in the refrigerator overnight. Strain it through a fine filter in the morning and you have a smooth, low acid concentrate that keeps for about a week and can be diluted with water, milk or ice to taste.

Our shop sits two streets from the harbour wall, in a brick building that once stored sailcloth. We kept the high beams and the long oak counter, added a small bar for tastings, and left the large front windows open to the morning light. On quiet days you can watch the ferries come in while the grinder hums behind you.

Espresso depends on four variables working together: dose, grind, time and temperature. Change one and the others must follow. A typical starting recipe is eighteen grams of coffee producing thirty six grams of liquid in about twenty eight seconds. Taste the result, then adjust the grind finer if it is sour and thin, or coarser if it is bitter and dry.

Training new baristas takes weeks of practice. They learn to taste before they pour, to clean the machine without being told, and to talk to customers about coffee without sounding like a lecture.
